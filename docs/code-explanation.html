1 Overview

This project is the firmware and control electronics for a wireless power transfer (WPT) transmitter. An ESP32 generates a 150 kHz switching waveform in hardware (MCPWM) and drives a half-bridge inverter through TLP250 gate drivers. The inverter feeds a series capacitor and the transmitter coil; a matching receiver coil picks the power up wirelessly. An actual electromechanical relay — controlled by GPIO13 through Optocoupler 1 — connects and disconnects the 12 V feed to the power stage. The relay's 5 V coil is powered by a 7805 linear regulator that steps the 12 V rail down to 5 V.

The system can run stand-alone: an IR obstacle sensor (GPIO27) detects an object in front of the coil, the firmware moves a servo (GPIO25) to 45° and starts wireless charging; when the object leaves, the servo returns to 0° and charging stops. A 16×2 I²C LCD shows status, and a 115200 baud serial console accepts commands.

Hardware revision note Earlier project documentation described a perfboard “supply module” whose IRF840 MOSFET acted as the GPIO13-controlled switch. That arrangement ran hot and behaved like a boost converter. This document describes the current revision: a real relay (5 V coil, driven via Optocoupler 1) gated by GPIO13, with the 7805 making 5 V from the 12 V rail for the relay coil. The firmware logic is unchanged — GPIO13 still means “supply ON/OFF”, so relay active-high semantics match the previous switch semantics.

Because the relay contacts are in series with the +V bus, an open relay galvanically separates the power stage from the 12 V supply. The 7805 taps the 12 V rail before the relay contact, so the relay coil circuit is always powered — the firmware can always close the relay when commanded.

Design intent The relay is not a 150 kHz device. It is a slow, low-loss supply gate (milliseconds). All fast switching happens in the MOSFET half bridge, which is gated by the MCPWM hardware at 150 kHz. The firmware keeps these two timescales strictly ordered (see the sequence timelines in Algorithms).

Boot-time caution GPIO13 and GPIO14 are JTAG pins and are not under firmware control during reset/boot. Fit external ~10 kΩ pull-downs on GPIO13, GPIO14 and GPIO26 so the relay stays de-energized and both gates stay LOW through every boot and reset.

3.2 Relay + 7805 connection list (working reference)

  1. 12 V feed: PSU + → fuse → relay COM; relay NO → +V bus of the half bridge (Q1 drain / bus capacitor bank).
  2. 7805 input: PSU + (before the relay) → 7805 IN. 0.33 µF from IN to GND.
  3. 7805 output: 7805 OUT → 5 V rail. 0.1 µF (plus a small electrolytic if desired) from OUT to GND. Mount on a heatsink if it runs warm (see thermal math).
  4. Opto LED side: GPIO13 → 220 Ω → PC817 anode (pin 1); PC817 cathode (pin 2) → GND.
  5. Opto output side: 5 V → PC817 collector (pin 4); PC817 emitter (pin 3) → 2N2222 base; 10 kΩ from base to GND (holds the transistor off, speeds turn-off).
  6. Coil driver: 2N2222 emitter → GND; 2N2222 collector → relay coil (−). Coil (+) → 5 V rail.
  7. Flyback diode: 1N4007 across the coil, cathode to +5 V, anode to the collector. Mandatory — the coil's inductive kick can destroy the driver and the optocoupler.
  8. Grounds: ESP32 GND, opto/driver GND, 7805 GND, relay-driver GND and PSU − are common. (With a shared ground the opto provides level shifting and pins protection, not full galvanic isolation.)
Using a ready-made relay module instead If you use an opto-isolated relay module with an IN pin, drive IN from GPIO13 and keep an active-high trigger so the firmware semantics (GPIO13 HIGH = relay energized) stay correct. The discrete PC817 + 2N2222 circuit above is the reference implementation whose numbers are computed in §8.

4.2 How the relay is driven (active-high chain)

GPIO13 HIGH → current flows through the opto LED → phototransistor conducts → base current into the 2N2222 → transistor saturates → the 5 V coil energizes → contacts close → 12 V reaches the +V bus. GPIO13 LOW removes all of it: coil de-energizes, contacts open, bus isolated. The firmware's safe state (relay OFF at boot, FaultLocked, and stop) therefore means “coil unpowered”, which is also the fail-safe state if the ESP32 resets (with external pull-downs fitted).

4.3 Why a 7805 from the 12 V rail

  • The relay has a 5 V coil (the most common, cheapest class), and the 12 V rail is already present — a 7805 gives the 5 V with two capacitors and no magnetics.
  • The load is small (~71 mA), so the linear regulator's loss (~0.5 W) is acceptable; a switching module would add EMI next to a 150 kHz power stage for no real benefit.
  • The 7805 also feeds the opto/driver stage, keeping all relay-side electronics on one regulated rail.
Do not power the servo from this 7805 The servo's stall current (~650 mA) would push the 7805 dissipation to (12−5) × 0.65 ≈ 4.6 W — far beyond what a TO-220 7805 can shed without a large heatsink. Keep the servo on its own 5 V supply as set out in the project notes.

4.4 Relay timing vs the firmware's waits

Protection limits An electromechanical relay takes milliseconds to open. It cannot protect the MOSFETs from overcurrent. Full-power operation still requires a fuse and a fast overcurrent shutdown (e.g., the MCPWM fault input with a one-shot trip). Never rely on GPIO13 as a protection device.

5 Power Stage Context (what the relay feeds)

The relay's NO contact delivers 12 V to a half-bridge inverter. Q1 (high side) has its drain on +V bus; the switch node between Q1 source and Q2 drain feeds a series capacitor and the transmitter coil. Each MOSFET has its own TLP250 gate driver, driven from GPIO14 (pair A) and GPIO26 (pair B).

The firmware side of the power stage is the MCPWM configuration explained in §7 and its mathematics in §8. The relay revision does not change any of this — it only changes how the 12 V feed is gated.

6.1 What the firmware is responsible for

  1. Generating the 150 kHz waveform in MCPWM hardware — never with digitalWrite() or software timing.
  2. Enforcing safe ordering at start/stop/fault: GPIO13 relay, gate pins, dead time, and the MCPWM timer are always sequenced in a fixed, safe order.
  3. Running the controller state machine that owns the relay and PWM states (7 states, single owner, no scattered flags).
  4. Automatic operation from the IR sensor: object → servo 45° + charge; object leaves → servo 0° + stop.
  5. Operator feedback: serial status/commands and the 16×2 LCD; safety-relevant state is always visible.

6.2 Execution model

Cooperative, non-blocking: the Arduino loop() runs five tasks in sequence, each cheap enough to return quickly (worst case is the ~40 ms LCD I²C refresh every 500 ms). The 150 kHz waveform is fully autonomous in hardware, so loop latency never disturbs it. 

void loop() {
  serviceSerial();       // parse console commands
  serviceIrControl();    // IR debounce + auto run/stop + servo
  serviceStateMachine(); // timed transitions (relay/PWM sequencing)
  updateLcd();           // throttled status display
  delay(1);              // yield
}

7 Code Walkthrough — src/main.cpp

7.1 Pin map and hardware constants

// Pin map from the requested wiring.
constexpr gpio_num_t kRelayPin = GPIO_NUM_13;   // Optocoupler 1: relay only
constexpr gpio_num_t kSwitchPinA = GPIO_NUM_14; // TLP250 2 and 3: diagonal pair A
constexpr gpio_num_t kSwitchPinB = GPIO_NUM_26; // TLP250 4 and 5: diagonal pair B

Three output pins matter to the power path: kRelayPin (the electromechanical relay through Optocoupler 1), and the two gate-command pins routed to MCPWM0A/MCPWM0B. (The “diagonal pair” wording in the comments dates from an earlier full-bridge draft; in the current half-bridge the firmware treats A as the high side and B as the low side — the firmware only cares that A and B are complementary and drive opposite switches.) All other pins: IR sensor, servo, LCD I²C.

7.2 MCPWM clocking — why 80 MHz

The waveform frequency resolution comes from the clock fed to the MCPWM timer. The legacy driver defaults to a 10 MHz group / 1 MHz timer clock, which would make a 150 kHz request become just 6 timer ticks — rounding to 166.7 kHz. The firmware instead runs both group and timer at 80 MHz, giving 533 ticks per 150 kHz period (see §8.1 for the arithmetic). 

// MCPWM clocking (ESP-IDF v4.4.7 legacy driver, Arduino-ESP32 2.0.17).
// The driver default is group 10 MHz / timer 1 MHz, which turns a 150 kHz
// request into peak = 1 MHz / 150 kHz = 6 ticks -> 166.7 kHz. Run both at
// 80 MHz instead: peak = 533 ticks -> 150.09 kHz with ~0.28 kHz tuning steps.
constexpr uint32_t kMcpwmBaseClockHz = 160000000UL; // SOC_MCPWM_BASE_CLK_HZ
constexpr uint32_t kMcpwmGroupResolutionHz = 80000000UL;
constexpr uint32_t kMcpwmTimerResolutionHz = 80000000UL;
static_assert(kMcpwmBaseClockHz % kMcpwmGroupResolutionHz == 0,
              "Group resolution must divide the 160 MHz MCPWM base clock");
static_assert(kMcpwmGroupResolutionHz % kMcpwmTimerResolutionHz == 0,
              "Timer resolution must divide the group resolution");
  • static_asserts make an invalid clock tree a compile error, not a run-time surprise.
  • Frequency bounds: kMinSwitchingFrequencyHz = 100 kHz, kMaxSwitchingFrequencyHz = 200 kHz; default 150 kHz, 50 % duty on both outputs.
  • A static_assert also proves the 16-bit period register can reach 100 kHz (80 MHz / 100 kHz = 800 < 65536).

7.3 Dead time — the most safety-critical constant

Q1 and Q2 must never conduct at the same time (that would short the 12 V bus). Dead time inserts a guaranteed both-OFF gap at every transition. It is expressed in MCPWM group clock ticks (12.5 ns at 80 MHz), taken from worst-case TLP250 propagation delay (0.5 µs max) plus IRF840 turn-off: 

constexpr uint32_t kDeadTimeNs = 700UL;
constexpr uint32_t kDeadTimeTicks = static_cast<uint32_t>(
    (static_cast<uint64_t>(kDeadTimeNs) * kMcpwmGroupResolutionHz +
     999999999ULL) /
    1000000000ULL);
static_assert(kDeadTimeTicks >= 1 && kDeadTimeTicks < 65535UL,
              "Dead-time register is 16 bits");
static_assert(kDeadTimeNs * 4UL < 1000000000UL / kMaxSwitchingFrequencyHz,
              "Dead time must stay well below half of the shortest period");

700 ns → 56 ticks (hardware delay likely 57 ticks ≈ 712.5 ns, per the driver source). At 150 kHz each half period is 3.33 µs, so each MOSFET still conducts ~2.62 µs per half cycle — see §8.2. The second static_assert guarantees the dead time stays far below half of the shortest period even at the 200 kHz maximum frequency.

7.4 Relay and sequence timing constants

// Relays move in milliseconds, while the inverter switches in microseconds.
// These are conservative defaults for bench bring-up; use the relay datasheet.
constexpr uint32_t kPwmPrechargeMs = 20UL;
constexpr uint32_t kRelaySettleMs = 50UL;
constexpr uint32_t kRelayDropoutMs = 80UL;

These three numbers order the two timescales: start the waveform before applying bus voltage (20 ms), wait out the relay's mechanical closure and contact bounce (50 ms), and after commanding the relay OFF wait out release time before removing gate drive (80 ms). The budget vs the relay datasheet is tabulated in §4.4.

7.5 Safe GPIO configuration at boot

Boot must not glitch the relay or the gates. The pins are pre-latched LOW before the output drivers are enabled, configured with internal pull-downs, and set to GPIO_MODE_INPUT_OUTPUT so the firmware can read back the real pad level of the relay pin (an output-only pad reads 0 and would misreport the relay state). 

void configureSafeGpio() {
  // Latch LOW in the output register before enabling the output drivers.
  gpio_set_level(kRelayPin, 0);
  gpio_set_level(kSwitchPinA, 0);
  gpio_set_level(kSwitchPinB, 0);

  // INPUT_OUTPUT so gpio_get_level() reads back the real pad level for the
  // relay status; with output-only mode the input path is off and reads 0.
  const gpio_config_t output_config = {
      .pin_bit_mask = (1ULL << kRelayPin) | (1ULL << kSwitchPinA) |
                      (1ULL << kSwitchPinB),
      .mode = GPIO_MODE_INPUT_OUTPUT,
      .pull_up_en = GPIO_PULLUP_DISABLE,
      .pull_down_en = GPIO_PULLDOWN_ENABLE,
      .intr_type = GPIO_INTR_DISABLE,
  };

  gpio_config(&output_config);
}

Note that GPIO13 LOW = opto LED OFF = 2N2222 OFF = relay coil de-energized = contacts open = bus isolated. This active-high chain is what makes the safe state truly safe. The separate gate-drive polarity is guarded by a compile-time check: 

static_assert(kGateCommandActiveHigh,
              "Safe state forces switch pins LOW; inverted gate drive needs a "
              "redesigned safe state before this can be changed");

7.6 Starting and stopping the waveform — ordering that prevents shoot-through

mcpwm_init() starts the timer with the dead-time module still in bypass — at that moment outputs A and B would be identical, i.e. both half-bridge switches ON (a bus short). The firmware therefore configures everything with the pins as plain GPIO LOW, enables dead time, waits for it to latch, and only then routes the pins to the MCPWM peripheral: 

  // Configure MCPWM completely while the pins are still plain GPIO LOW.
  // mcpwm_init() starts the timer itself, and the dead-time module resets to
  // bypass, so A and B are briefly identical (both HIGH = both diagonal pairs
  // ON). Routing the pins only after dead time is active avoids that window.
  if (!checkEsp(mcpwm_init(kMcpwmUnit, kMcpwmTimer, &pwm_config),
                "mcpwm_init")) {
    return false;
  }
  g_switchingActive = true; // timer is running internally from here on

  if (!checkEsp(mcpwm_deadtime_enable(kMcpwmUnit, kMcpwmTimer,
                                      MCPWM_ACTIVE_HIGH_COMPLIMENT_MODE,
                                      kDeadTimeTicks, kDeadTimeTicks),
                "mcpwm_deadtime_enable")) {
    return false;
  }

  delayMicroseconds(kMcpwmSettleUs);   // 50 us: dead-time delays latch at a timer zero

  if (!checkEsp(mcpwm_gpio_init(kMcpwmUnit, MCPWM0A, kSwitchPinA),
                "mcpwm_gpio_init A")) {
    return false;
  }
  if (!checkEsp(mcpwm_gpio_init(kMcpwmUnit, MCPWM0B, kSwitchPinB),
                "mcpwm_gpio_init B")) {
    return false;
  }

Shutdown does the reverse and is equally strict: detach the pins and force them LOW first (via forceSwitchPinsLowAsGpio(), a straight GPIO reconfiguration that cannot be blocked by MCPWM state — the gate-kill path), then stop the timer and disable dead time. The relay itself is switched before that, with waits, per the timelines in §9.

7.7 Fault latch — fail safe, then stay safe

Every MCPWM API return code is checked by checkEsp(). Any failure calls latchFault(), which removes the relay and the gate drive immediately — it does not wait for relay dropout, because when something is wrong, killing gate drive takes priority: 

// Latches until reset. Relay OFF and gates OFF immediately, without waiting
// for relay dropout: on a fault, removing gate drive takes priority.
void latchFault(const char *reason) {
  relayOff();
  forceSwitchPinsLowAsGpio();
  if (g_switchingActive) {
    mcpwm_stop(kMcpwmUnit, kMcpwmTimer);
    g_switchingActive = false;
  }
  g_faultReason = reason;
  enterState(ControllerState::FaultLocked);
  Serial.printf("FAULT LATCHED: %s. Outputs safe; reset required.\n", reason);
}

FaultLocked is sticky: nothing but a reset clears it. The LCD shows FAULT - RESET plus the reason, and status reports it on the console.

7.8 The controller state machine

enum class ControllerState : uint8_t {
  Idle,               // outputs safely off
  StartingPwm,        // waveform live, waiting 20 ms before relay ON
  WaitingRelaySettle, // relay ON, waiting 50 ms for contact settling
  Running,            // powered transfer
  StoppingRelayDropout,// relay OFF, waiting 80 ms before killing gate drive
  FaultLocked         // sticky fault, outputs safe, reset required
};

The timed transitions live in serviceStateMachine(), which compares millis() - g_stateEnteredMs against the constants each loop pass: 

  switch (g_state) {
  case ControllerState::StartingPwm:
    if (elapsed >= kPwmPrechargeMs) {
      relayOn();
      enterState(ControllerState::WaitingRelaySettle);
      Serial.println("Relay energized; waiting for contact settling.");
    }
    break;

  case ControllerState::WaitingRelaySettle:
    if (elapsed >= kRelaySettleMs) {
      enterState(ControllerState::Running);
      Serial.println("RUNNING.");
    }
    break;

  case ControllerState::StoppingRelayDropout:
    if (elapsed >= kRelayDropoutMs) {
      stopSwitchingOutputs();
      if (!isFaulted()) {
        enterState(ControllerState::Idle);
        Serial.println("Stopped: relay OFF, PWM OFF, switch pins LOW.");
      }
    }
    break;

No delays block the loop — each state simply notes when it was entered and acts when its time has elapsed.

7.9 Serial console

serviceSerial() accumulates characters until a line ending; lines are trimmed, lower-cased and dispatched. The buffer is capped at 48 characters — longer input is discarded with a message rather than overflowing. Harmless commands (status, help, stop) work in any state; freq is refused unless the controller is Idle. There is no manual turn-on command: the IR sensor is the only way to start (the IR-triggered run also uses requireIdle): 

bool requireIdle(const char *command) {
  if (g_state == ControllerState::Idle) {
    return true;
  }
  Serial.printf("Refusing %s: controller is not Idle. Send 'stop' first.\n",
                command);
  return false;
}
  • The IR-triggered run additionally requires the two compile-time arm flags (kPowerStageTopologyVerified and kAllowRelayEnergizedRun, both currently true — armed 2026-10-11 at the user's request; not scope-verified).
  • freq <hz> only changes the requested frequency while Idle, because the legacy driver applies a new period immediately — changing it mid-run could stretch a switching cycle. The console reports both the requested and the actual frequency:
uint32_t actualFrequencyHz(const uint32_t requested_hz) {
  // Mirrors mcpwm_init(): peak = timer_clk / frequency (integer division).
  return kMcpwmTimerResolutionHz / (kMcpwmTimerResolutionHz / requested_hz);
}

7.10 IR sensor, servo and automatic operation

The IR module (FC-51 class, powered from 3.3 V) pulls GPIO27 LOW when an object is detected. The firmware debounces the raw signal (100 ms), waits 2 s after boot before acting, and executes exactly once per stable change: 

  if (now >= kIrStartDelayMs && now - raw_since_ms >= kIrDebounceMs &&
      (!have_state || raw != g_irDetecting)) {
    have_state = true;
    g_irDetecting = raw;
    if (g_irDetecting) {
      Serial.println("IR on: servo 45 deg, transfer ON.");
      setServoAngle(kServoTransferAngleDeg);
      run_pending = true;
    } else {
      Serial.println("IR off: servo 0 deg, transfer OFF.");
      setServoAngle(kServoIdleAngleDeg);
      run_pending = false;
      stopController();
    }
  }

On detection the servo moves to 45° and a run_pending flag requests the powered start. The flag is re-tried each loop until the state machine reaches Idle — this handles the case “object returns while the previous stop sequence is still waiting for relay dropout” without ever violating requireIdle(): 

  // run only starts from Idle, e.g. after a quick IR off -> on while the
  // stop sequence is still waiting for relay dropout.
  if (run_pending && g_state == ControllerState::Idle) {
    run_pending = false;
    requestPoweredRun();
  }

A serial stop is not overridden by the IR input — it holds until the IR state changes again. Servo pulses are generated with LEDC at 50 Hz, 16-bit, mapped linearly from 500–2500 µs (0–180°): 

void setServoAngle(const uint32_t degrees) {
  const uint32_t pulse_us =
      kServoMinPulseUs + (kServoMaxPulseUs - kServoMinPulseUs) * degrees / 180UL;
  const uint32_t period_us = 1000000UL / kServoFrequencyHz;
  const uint32_t max_duty = (1UL << kServoResolutionBits) - 1UL;
  ledcWrite(kServoLedcChannel, pulse_us * max_duty / period_us);
  g_servoAngleDeg = degrees;
}

7.11 LCD status

A 16×2 I²C LCD (address 0x27) refreshes every 500 ms — status only, it can never gate control. Line 1 shows the state and relay contact state (read back from the pad), line 2 the actual frequency and whether MCPWM is active: 

    snprintf(line1, sizeof(line1), "%-10s R:%-3s", stateShortName(g_state),
             gpio_get_level(kRelayPin) ? "ON" : "OFF");
    snprintf(line2, sizeof(line2), "%3" PRIu32 ".%02" PRIu32 "kHz SW:%-3s",
             hz / 1000U, (hz % 1000U) / 10U, g_switchingActive ? "ON" : "OFF");

Example display while charging: line 1 RUNNING   R:ON , line 2 150.09kHz SW:ON . If faulted, line 1 becomes FAULT - RESET and line 2 the fault reason. The library's init() blocks ~1 s once during setup(); each refresh costs ~40 ms on the loop — acceptable because relay and IR events live on millisecond scales.

7.12 setup() — establishing the safe state first

  1. Start Serial (115200).
  2. configureSafeGpio() — relay OFF, gate pins LOW before their drivers are enabled.
  3. stopSwitchingOutputs() — guarantee MCPWM is detached/off even after an abnormal reset.
  4. Idle state, then banner: “Safe state: relay OFF, switching outputs inactive.”
  5. IR pin pull-up, LEDC + servo attached at 0°, LCD initialised.
  6. Print help and status; note that IR control starts 2 s after boot (so a present object starts charging by itself).

8 Calculations

Every number below is either taken from src/main.cpp constants or derived; relay/regulator numbers use typical datasheet values for the components named.

8.1 MCPWM: period ticks and actual frequency

Period in timer ticks (integer division, as the driver does) N = ⌊ftimer ⁄ frequest⌋ = ⌊80 000 000 ⁄ 150 000⌋ = ⌊533.33⌋ = 533 ticks
Actual switching frequency factual = ftimer ⁄ N = 80 000 000 ⁄ 533 = 150 093.8 Hz ≈ 150.09 kHz
Period and half-period (1 tick = 1 ⁄ 80 MHz = 12.5 ns) T = 533 × 12.5 ns = 6.6625 µs   ·   T ⁄ 2 = 3.33125 µs
Frequency tuning step (one tick) Δf ≈ f² ⁄ ftimer = (150 000)² ⁄ 80 000 000 ≈ 281 Hz ≈ 0.28 kHz per step

Sanity limit: the period register is 16 bits, so the slowest waveform this clock can make is 80 MHz ⁄ 65 535 ≈ 1.22 kHz — the firmware's 100 kHz floor is far inside the range. The LCD shows the actual frequency (150 094 → “150.09kHz”), and status prints requested and actual side by side.

8.2 Dead time: ticks, real delay, and conduction time

Ticks (group clock = 80 MHz → 12.5 ns/tick) ticks = ⌈tdt × fgroup⌉ = ⌈700 ns × 80 MHz⌉ = 56 ticks
Delay the hardware likely produces (driver programs ticks + 1) tdt,hw ≈ (56 + 1) × 12.5 ns = 712.5 ns
Conduction time per MOSFET per half cycle tcond = T ⁄ 2 − tdt,hw = 3331.25 ns − 712.5 ns = 2618.75 ns ≈ 2.62 µs (≈ 78.6 % of the half period)
Why 700 ns? Worst-case budget TLP250 propagation: 0.15 µs typ, 0.5 µs max → opto-to-opto skew can approach 0.5 µs; IRF840 turn-off (td(off) + tf) ≈ 69 ns. 700 ns ≥ worst-case skew + turn-off, with margin.

The compile-time guard kDeadTimeNs × 4 < 1 ⁄ fmax checks 2.8 µs < 5 µs, i.e. even at 200 kHz the dead time is less than a quarter of a half-cycle. Reduce these values only after measuring gate-to-source waveforms on real hardware.

8.3 Start/stop timing budget (firmware waits vs relay mechanics)

Precharge (PWM before bus) 20 ms ÷ 6.6625 µs ≈ 3000 switching periods of waveform before the relay may close — MCPWM and dead time fully settled, no first-cycle glitch reaches a powered bridge.
Contact settle after coil ON 50 ms ≥ 4× typical operate time (5–10 ms) + contact bounce (1–3 ms) → state Running only after the contacts are electrically quiet.
Dropout before gate drive is removed 80 ms ≥ 8× typical release time (≤ 10 ms) → when stopSwitchingOutputs() runs, the +V bus is already isolated and decaying.

8.4 Relay coil current, 7805 dissipation and thermals

Using a typical 5 V class relay (e.g. SRD-05VDC-SL-C class: coil ≈ 70 Ω):

Coil current and power Icoil = 5 V ⁄ 70 Ω = 71.4 mA   ·   Pcoil = 5 V × 71.4 mA ≈ 0.36 W
7805 dissipation (linear regulator: the voltage drop becomes heat) P7805 = (Vin − Vout) × Iload = (12 − 5) × 0.0714 ≈ 0.50 W (plus ~0.03–0.06 W quiescent)
Junction temperature rise, TO-220 with no heatsink (θJA ≈ 65 °C/W) ΔT ≈ P × θJA = 0.50 × 65 ≈ 33 °C above ambient → e.g. ~63 °C junction at 30 °C ambient (safe, but warm)

Other 7805 checks: dropout (~2 V) — 12 V input is comfortably above the ~7.5 V minimum; max input 35 V; add the datasheet capacitors (0.33 µF in, 0.1 µF out) to keep it stable. If the 12 V rail runs at 13–14 V, every number above scales up (~0.55–0.65 W) — still fine for the relay-only load.

8.5 Optocoupler LED, CTR budget and relay-driver base current

Opto LED series resistor (GPIO 3.3 V, LED Vf ≈ 1.2 V) R = (VGPIO − Vf) ⁄ If = (3.3 − 1.2) ⁄ 9.5 mA ≈ 221 Ω → use 220 Ω; with 220 Ω, If ≈ 9.5 mA (well inside the GPIO's source capability and the opto's rating)
Current available on the phototransistor side (CTR ≥ 50 % worst case) Ic,opto = CTR × If ≥ 0.5 × 9.5 mA ≈ 4.7 mA
Base current the 2N2222 actually needs (hFE,min ≈ 100 at 70 mA) Ib,min = Icoil ⁄ hFE = 71.4 mA ⁄ 100 ≈ 0.71 mA → the opto can supply ≥ 6× that, so the transistor is driven deep into saturation
Transistor loss and contact loss PQ ≈ VCE(sat) × IC ≈ 0.25 V × 71.4 mA ≈ 18 mW  ·  Pcontact ≈ 50 mΩ × Ibus² (≈ 0.2 W at 2 A — vs several watts in the old IRF840 module)

8.6 Servo PWM duty calculation (LEDC)

Counts per microsecond at 50 Hz / 16-bit period = 1 ⁄ 50 Hz = 20 000 µs  ·  counts/µs = 65 536 ⁄ 20 000 = 3.2768
Pulse → duty count (duty = pulse_us × 65 535 ⁄ 20 000, integer) 0° → 500 µs → 1638  ·  45° → 1000 µs → 3276  ·  180° → 2500 µs → 8191
Resolution of the angle 1 count = 0.305 µs ≈ 0.0275° — far finer than the servo's own dead band.

The LEDC divider 80 MHz ⁄ (50 Hz × 65 536) = 24 + 106/256 is exactly representable by the hardware divider, so the 50 Hz frame rate is exact; 45° is produced as 500 + 2000 × 45 ⁄ 180 = 1000 µs.

8.7 Power-stage resonance (context for what the relay feeds)

Series capacitor for L ≈ 79 µH at 150 kHz C = 1 ⁄ ((2π × 150 kHz)² × 79 µH) ≈ 1 ⁄ (8.883 × 10¹¹ × 79 × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 14.25 nF → ~14.2 nF (e.g. 10 nF + 4.7 nF film, ≥ 630 V DC)

Hazard reminder (design notes, calculated not measured): at resonance with no receiver, ~5.1 A rms in the loop and ~380 V rms across the capacitor. Commission at 2–3 V, current-limited, receiver present; operate slightly above resonance to keep the IRF840 body diodes out of hard recovery.

9.1 Main loop — cooperative round-robin

9.3 Run sequence — timeline (with the actual relay)

  • t = 0 ms — IR-on run accepted. Checks: requireIdle() and both arm flags. Relay OFF, gate pins LOW.
  • t ≈ 0 ms — startSwitchingOutputs(). Pins stay GPIO-LOW → clocks set to 80 MHz → mcpwm_init() (timer runs, dead time still bypassed) → mcpwm_deadtime_enable() → wait 50 µs (≈ 7.5 periods) for the delays to latch → route GPIO14/GPIO26 to MCPWM0A/0B last. The 150 kHz waveform is now live, but the bus is dead.
  • t = 20 ms — relay coil energized. GPIO13 HIGH through Optocoupler 1 and the NPN driver; contacts close ~5–10 ms later; 12 V appears on the +V bus while a clean waveform is already running. State: WaitingRelaySettle.
  • t = 70 ms — Running. Contact bounce is over; the bridge is transferring power to the TX coil.

9.4 Stop sequence — timeline

  • t = 0 ms — stop / IR-off. Relay coil de-energized first: contacts open within ~5–10 ms; the +V bus is now isolated and decaying while the gates keep switching harmlessly. State: StoppingRelayDropout.
  • t = 80 ms — gate drive removed. forceSwitchPinsLowAsGpio() (detach pins from MCPWM and force LOW) → mcpwm_stop() → mcpwm_deadtime_disable(). State: Idle.

9.5 Fault path — immediate

fault(reason):
  relayOff()                  # coil off right now — no 80 ms courtesy
  forceSwitchPinsLowAsGpio()  # gates LOW right now — supersedes all sequencing
  mcpwm_stop()                # if the timer was running
  state = FaultLocked         # sticky, survives until reset
  print reason to serial + LCD

Rationale: on a fault the priority order flips — removing gate drive matters more than waiting for the relay to drop out. The relay is still commanded OFF in the same instant, so both energy paths die immediately.

9.6 IR control — debounce and auto run/stop

every loop pass:
  raw = (digitalRead(GPIO27) == LOW)        # active-low sensor
  if raw changed: raw_since = now           # restart debounce timer

  if now ≥ 2000 ms (boot delay)
     and raw stable ≥ 100 ms
     and raw ≠ reported_state:
        reported_state = raw
        if raw:  servo = 45°; run_pending = true      # object present
        else:    servo = 0°; run_pending = false; stopController()

  if run_pending and state == Idle:
        run_pending = false; requestPoweredRun()      # retry-safe
  • Acts once per stable change, plus once at boot if an object is already present (after the 2 s delay).
  • A manual stop while IR is on is respected: the firmware won't restart until the IR state changes.
  • The run_pending retry closes the race “IR returns while the stop sequence is still finishing”.

9.7 Serial command parsing

  trim + lowercase
  "stop"   → always allowed → relay-first shutdown
  "freq N" → requireIdle + range check 100 000..200 000 → store requested Hz
  "status" → print state/fault/relay/switching/freq/dead time/IR/servo/flags
  "help"   → command help

9.8 Frequency change — why Idle-only

The legacy MCPWM driver writes the period register immediately; changing it on a running timer can stretch one switching cycle. freq therefore only stores the request while Idle, and the new value is applied by the next IR-triggered start. The console and LCD always show the recomputed actual frequency, not the requested one (150 000 requested → 150.09 kHz actual at 80 MHz).

There is no manual turn-on command. IR-on (Idle + arm flags) runs the powered sequence: PWM → 20 ms → relay ON → 50 ms → Running. IR-off calls stop. A serial stop is not reversed by the IR state until the IR input changes.

11 Safety, Verification Status & Operating Limits

11.1 Guaranteed safe behaviours (by construction)

  • Boot: relay coil de-energized (GPIO13 LOW, latched before drivers enable), both gate pins LOW, MCPWM off; stopSwitchingOutputs() re-enforced at startup.
  • No shoot-through window: dead time is enabled and settled before the pins are routed to MCPWM; pins are detached and forced LOW before the timer is stopped.
  • Faults fail safe: any MCPWM error latches FaultLocked — relay OFF and gates LOW immediately, sticky until reset.
  • No unsolicited power: the IR-triggered run requires compile-time arm flags, and there is no serial turn-on command.
  • Polarity guard: a static_assert blocks building with inverted gate command, because the safe state (LOW) assumes active-high gate drive.

11.2 Not verified on hardware (as of 2026-10-12)

  • Dead time (700 ns / ~712.5 ns) has not been confirmed on a scope; the TLP250 delays and IRF840 turn-off are datasheet values.
  • The arm flags are true (recorded 2026-10-11 at user request) but no gate-to-source waveform, bus current, or thermal measurement exists.
  • The relay + 7805 revision itself: coil current, contact drop, 7805 temperature, and the 20/50/80 ms windows are calculated, not measured (relay datasheet values assumed).
  • High-side TLP250 floating supply (bootstrap / floating 15 V) has never been confirmed.
  • No oscilloscope capture of the first switching cycle exists; the “never both HIGH” claim follows from the driver sequence, not from measurement.

11.3 Required protections before full power

  • Fuse in the 12 V feed (before the relay contact), sized to the expected bus current.
  • Fast overcurrent shutdown (e.g. MCPWM fault input with one-shot trip). The relay is millisecond-scale and cannot protect MOSFETs — it gates the supply, it is not the protection.
  • External ~10 kΩ pull-downs on GPIO13/14/26 (JTAG pins are uncontrolled during boot).
  • Servo on its own 5 V supply with shared ground; never on the relay's 7805 (see §8.4).
  • LCD I²C pull-up review: backpack modules often pull SDA/SCL to 5 V — use 3.3 V-safe pull-ups or a level shifter.
  • Commission at 2–3 V, current-limited, receiver present; watch for the ~380 V rms / ~5.1 A rms no-receiver resonance hazard.
Operating limits inherited from the design notes The IRF840 (0.85–1.5 Ω) is a poor fit for a 12 V bus — low-RDS(on) parts (e.g. IRFZ44N class) are recommended. Run slightly above resonance. Keep the relay contact within its DC rating (typical small relays: 10 A / 30 V DC — verify yours). The relay is an on/off supply gate only; nothing in this firmware makes it fast.
